Urban trees change the small patch of climate around them. A tree canopy shades pavement, reducing the sunlight that reaches the surface. Leaves also release water vapour through transpiration. Evaporation of that water uses heat, which helps cool the surrounding air. Shade and transpiration are different cooling processes.

Tree roots and the soil around them help manage rainfall. Loose soil allows water to soak into the ground, a process called infiltration. Compacted soil contains fewer open spaces, so less water can infiltrate. Mulch protects the soil surface and slows the loss of moisture. Keep mulch away from the trunk to avoid trapping moisture against the bark.

Choosing a tree requires looking beyond its current size. Its mature canopy needs room above the ground, and its roots need room below it. A species suited to local rainfall usually needs less additional watering once established. Newly planted trees still need regular care while their roots grow into the surrounding soil.

A varied mix of tree species can make an urban forest more resilient. If every street has the same species, a pest that attacks that species can damage many trees at once. Diversity spreads that risk. It does not mean that every species is suitable for every street.

Monitoring helps a community learn whether planting is working. Useful observations include survival rates, canopy cover and soil moisture. Counting newly planted trees alone does not show how many survive. Comparing observations across several seasons gives a clearer picture than a single measurement.
